What really helps against acne?

INGREDIENTS · ~7 MIN READ
Updated: May 2026

Acne isn't "dirty skin" and it isn't "food sin". It's a medical issue with clear causes. Here are the ingredients that actually convince in studies — and which ones are just marketing.

⚠️ Note: This article is for general information purposes only and does not replace professional medical or dermatological advice. If you have skin problems, allergies, or intolerances, please consult a doctor.

The most important question first

For severe acne (cystic, scarring, large-area, painful), the rule is: see a dermatologist. There are prescription treatments (isotretinoin, antibiotic creams, spironolactone) that are significantly more powerful than anything in the drugstore. Over-the-counter products are great for mild to moderate acne — but not a substitute for medical treatment in severe cases.

The "Big Four" of acne ingredients

1. Salicylic acid (BHA)

A fat-soluble acid that penetrates pores and cleans them from inside. Especially good for blackheads (comedones) and blemished skin. Usual concentration: 0.5–2 %. Found in toners, cleansers and spot treatments.

When to use? In the evening, 2–3× per week at first. Not together with retinol on the same evening.

2. Benzoyl peroxide (BPO)

The classic against inflammatory pimples. Kills acne bacteria (Cutibacterium acnes). Fast and powerful — but can dry skin out and bleach towels (!). Usual concentration: 2.5 % (gentle, almost as effective as higher doses) or 5 %.

When to use? Spot-apply to inflamed pimples. Don't apply over large areas unless a doctor prescribes it that way.

💡 Benzoyl peroxide bleaches anything with pigment: dark towels, pillowcases, even T-shirts. Use light-coloured or dedicated towels.

3. Retinoids (retinol, adapalene)

Vitamin A derivatives that boost cell turnover and prevent comedones. Adapalene is now over-the-counter in Austria (e.g. Differin Gel) and the strongest you can get without a prescription.

When to use? In the evening, ease in slowly (start with 2× per week). Sunscreen the next morning is mandatory, because retinoids make skin more sensitive.

4. Azelaic acid

The underrated all-rounder. Works against acne, pigment spots and redness. Very well tolerated, also allowed during pregnancy. Usual concentration: 10 % (over-the-counter) to 20 % (prescription).

When to use? 1–2× daily. Combines well with other actives.

Supporting ingredients

Niacinamide

Vitamin B3. Regulates sebum production, reduces redness, visibly reduces pores. Very well tolerated. Concentration: 5 %. Can be used both in the morning and evening.

Zinc

Acts anti-inflammatory from outside (in creams) and also from within (as a dietary supplement, consult a doctor). Particularly helpful with inflammatory acne.

Tea tree oil

Antibacterial, slightly anti-inflammatory. Studies show effect at 5 % dilution, comparable to benzoyl peroxide — but gentler. Never apply undiluted, always dilute!

What to rather avoid

  • Aggressive cleansers / brushes / "deep cleansing": Mechanical scrubbing irritates the skin, often making acne worse. Gentle cleansing is better long-term.
  • Coconut oil, cocoa-based products, dense petroleum jelly: Highly comedogenic — can clog pores on acne-prone skin. Reach for non-comedogenic products instead (usually labelled).
  • Alcohol-based toners: Dry sebum short-term, but long-term the skin produces more sebum in response. Counterproductive.
  • Overdone routines: 8 steps a day with 5 actives is usually worse than 3 steps with one active. Less is more.

How long until something happens?

Patience. Acne treatments need 6–12 weeks before you see a clear difference. Some actives (especially retinoids) cause a purging effect in the first 4–6 weeks — the skin gets worse briefly before getting better. That's normal.

What's not normal: burning pain, severe redness, swelling. In that case: stop the product and consult a doctor if needed.

Diet and lifestyle

This is debated, but research leans towards three factors:

  • Milk & dairy products can trigger acne in some people (hormonal effect).
  • High sugar consumption (high glycaemic index) correlates with acne. Whole grains instead of white flour, less sweets.
  • Stress raises cortisol, which boosts sebum production. Sleep, exercise, breaks — not esoteric stuff.

But: diet alone rarely cures acne. It can support — topical treatment with actives remains the main work.
